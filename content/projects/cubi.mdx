## The customer experience

Customers pick a product format — **Cubi Base**, a compact counter-top NFC
stand; **Cubi Boards** with two, three or four tile positions; or **Cubi Lite**
as a cheaper entry point — then design it in a live 3D customiser rather than
filling in a form and imagining the result.

They control the board colour, tile colour, uploaded logo, custom text, font,
individual tile icons, NFC destinations, and the product's size and layout,
watching the model update in real time.

Two decisions kept it manufacturable:

- **A two-colour design system.** Customers choose the board colour and the
  tile colour; everything else is derived. This keeps products visually
  consistent and stops people building combinations that can't be printed.
- **An admin-managed filament system.** Customers only ever see colours that
  physically exist. Each filament carries a calibrated colour value matched to
  the real spool, and disabling one in the admin panel removes it from the
  storefront immediately.

## The image-to-SVG converter

The hardest part of the project. A customer should be able to upload almost any
image of their logo and get back a clean, printable shape — but real uploads are
low-resolution, blurry, screenshotted, dark-on-light, light-on-dark,
transparent, surrounded by empty space, multi-coloured, stroke-based, or unsafe
SVG files.

Rather than paying an external service, I built my own conversion workflow. It
measures contrast and sharpness, identifies the likely background, picks a
thresholding strategy, removes noise, trims empty borders, traces the contours
and emits a clean compound path.

For raster images the pipeline runs: validation and size limits, decode and
resize, contrast and sharpness analysis, transparency detection, background and
foreground analysis, Otsu thresholding where appropriate, dark-on-light and
light-on-dark candidate generation, noise cleanup, border removal, boundary
tracing, contour simplification, then normalisation and centring.

Uploaded SVGs go through a separate sanitisation pass that strips scripts, event
handlers, external references, embedded images, text, animations, filters,
gradients and styles, leaving only geometry to convert into a one-colour
silhouette.

The converter generates several candidates — readable, full-shape, dark, light,
cut-out — scores each against the original, and shows the customer the options
before continuing.

The final manufacturing SVG is deliberately boring and reliable: one compound
path, black fill, even-odd hole handling, a consistent `0 0 1000 1000` viewBox,
centred with controlled padding, and no external dependencies, scripts,
gradients or filters.

All of this runs locally in a Web Worker with WebAssembly-based tracing.
**Customer logo files are never sent to an external image-processing API.**

## From a logo to a physical object

The processed SVG feeds straight into the 3D viewer, built with React,
Three.js, React Three Fiber and Drei. It handles real-time previews,
product-specific mesh mapping, logo placement, tile and icon previews, dynamic
material colours and multiple board sizes across desktop and mobile.

The logo isn't treated as a flat image — it becomes part of the model, inset
into the board or assigned to a tile. I also added logo paint regions, so
selected parts of a logo can be assigned different physical colours.

The admin panel generates a 3MF package from a saved configuration that opens
directly in Bambu Studio. It has to handle the board, separate snap-fit tiles,
recessed logos, tile icons, multiple colours, product metadata, varying sizes,
malformed logo elements and stroke-based icons.

The approach I landed on uses separate single-colour mesh objects grouped
through the 3MF component system, so a board, logo or icon assembly appears as
one build item while keeping its colour separation intact. The customer's
design information — colours, text, font, icon and product configuration — is
embedded in the 3MF metadata.

## Backend and data

Firebase, deliberately kept on the Spark plan and designed around that limit.
Instead of putting raw images in Firebase Storage, the processed manufacturing
SVG is stored as text inside the Firestore order document, which keeps the
architecture light and the cost at zero.

Orders carry everything needed to recreate a design: customer and shipping
details, product selection, board and tile colours, text and font settings,
tile icons, the logo SVG, paint regions, design metadata and status. Uploaded
SVGs are validated and sanitised before acceptance, and order payloads have
limits on file size, item count and nesting.

Ordering currently works as an order request with payment handled offline —
that let me build and test the entire product and manufacturing workflow before
committing to a payment provider.

Cloudflare Workers handle hosting and deployment; pushes to `main` on GitHub
trigger the build, serving the live site at `cubi-3d.com`.

## The admin panel

The operational centre of the whole thing. Orders move through a kanban board:
new, waiting to print, printing, printed and waiting to ship, shipped,
delivered, cancelled.

Opening an order shows a **live 3D reconstruction of what the customer
designed**, so I can check colours, logo, text and icons before generating the
manufacturing file — then export the 3MF and open it in Bambu Studio without
rebuilding anything by hand.

The same panel controls what can be ordered at all: enabling or disabling
products and individual filaments, managing the customer-facing colour palette,
reviewing inventory, updating order statuses and reviewing logos before they go
live. Access is restricted to the owner account through Firebase auth and admin
security rules.

## Finding the first customers

To launch, I built an outreach system aimed at university societies. A scraper
collects society names, pages, email addresses, logos and useful links — around
**94 UCD societies** so far — and an internal review dashboard lets me inspect
and approve the scraped logos.

Each approved society gets its own page at `/for/society-name` showing a
personalised Cubi preview with their own logo already loaded. Instead of a
generic product link, outreach shows an organisation what its own branded
product would look like.

A campaign sync workflow pushes approved societies into a Google Sheet with
deduplication, opt-out handling and eligibility checks. The email side has
unsubscribe links, server-side opt-out recording, signed unsubscribe URLs,
duplicate prevention and contact validation built in from the start.

## Physical design

The models were built in Fusion: board and tile geometry, magnetic parts, NFC
components, recessed logo areas, snap-fit parts and printable mesh layouts —
designed around society stalls, committee rooms, reception desks, counters and
small businesses.

Cubi Base is compact and counter-top with two focused NFC destinations, easy to
carry and set up at a stall. The larger boards trade portability for more
branding space, more tap destinations, text and tile icons.

## Performance

A page with 3D models and in-browser image processing needs care. I code-split
the 3D functionality, lazy-load both the logo converter and the manufacturing
export engine, keep WebAssembly out of the initial bundle, mount canvases only
when needed, limit device pixel ratio, cache mesh-role detection, memoise
resolved product configurations and avoid unnecessary SVG texture rebuilds.

Tested on desktop and down to 390px mobile, specifically checking for
horizontal overflow, broken sticky controls, console errors, WebGL errors and
whether the customiser is actually usable on a phone.
