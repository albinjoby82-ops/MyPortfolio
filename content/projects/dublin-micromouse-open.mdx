A rulebook is mostly a list of decisions somebody had to make and then defend.
Setting up a national competition from nothing meant answering a stack of
questions that have very little to do with robots — what counts as a legal
machine, how a run is timed and verified, what happens when a mouse fails
mid-run, who pays for the parts, and how a complete beginner gets to the start
line without being embarrassed.

## Designing the format first

The format came before everything else, because it decides who can enter.

A months-long build favours teams who already have a workshop, a parts drawer and
someone who has done it before. Compressing the whole thing into six hours on a
single day, with an identical kit issued to every team, removes that advantage
entirely. It also changes what the competition actually measures: not who owns
the best equipment, but who can make decisions quickly and debug under pressure.

Everything else in the rulebook follows from that decision. The kit is fixed, so
the technical rules are about what you may do with it rather than what you may
bring. The main award goes to the fastest verified run to the centre of the maze,
with further awards for design and reliability, so a mouse that is quick but only
finishes once does not beat one that works every time.

## Making the kit modular on purpose

Every team receives the same eight-part kit: an ESP32, encoded motors, a motor
driver, an IMU, distance sensors, a battery management system, a buck converter
and a battery pack.

It is modular deliberately. There is no single correct mouse design, and the
expectation is that very different machines come out of the same box of parts —
different sensor placements, different chassis, different ideas about how much
to trust the encoders versus the IMU. A kit that assembled only one way would
have turned the day into a soldering exercise.

## Removing the excuses not to enter

"No experience needed" is easy to put on a poster and hard to actually deliver
in six hours. The preparation is built in three layers.

**Before the day.** A pre-recorded video series covering how to drive the sensors
with the ESP32, plus written documentation and setup docs published in advance,
so a team's first hour goes into the robot instead of into environment setup.

**On the day.** A 30-minute opening briefing with all the electronics diagrams
prepared in advance: how to use the encoded motors, the motor driver, the IMU and
the distance sensors, why there is a battery management system in the kit, why
there is a buck converter, and how the battery packs actually work. It also
covers GitHub basics for anyone who has not used it, and walks through the
different maze-solving strategies so teams pick an approach rather than guess at
one.

**Throughout.** ElecSoc committee members and postgraduate demonstrators on the
floor all day, so a stuck team has someone standing next to them within a minute.

## Funding it

The competition is supported by Anthropic, providing a credits package for the
winning team, and by the UCD School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering.

The sponsorship structure is three tiers that run together rather than compete: a
Presenting Event Partner carrying naming rights and the largest presence on every
surface, an Official Prize Partner whose contribution is ring-fenced entirely for
the prize pool, and up to three Official Technical Partners covering sensors,
motors, batteries, PCBs, filament, tools and printing. Support is accepted as
cash, product or in kind, which matters when the thing you need is a box of
distance sensors rather than a transfer.
