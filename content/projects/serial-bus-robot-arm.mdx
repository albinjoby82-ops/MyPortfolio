## Overview

This project started as an electronics and robotics project to learn how a
multi-axis robotic arm actually works beyond simply buying a finished robot.

I used an existing robot arm design available online and 3D printed the
mechanical components myself. I did not design the original mechanical
structure, but assembling, modifying and troubleshooting it gave me a much
better understanding of mechanical concepts such as joint geometry, linkages,
tolerances, servo mounting, range of motion and how the geometry of individual
joints affects the overall workspace of a robot.

The main part of the project for me was the electronics and control system.

The arm uses eight Feetech STS3215 serial bus servos connected through a
Waveshare Serial Bus Servo Driver and powered from a 12 V supply. Instead of
controlling each servo independently using conventional PWM signals, the
servos communicate over a shared serial bus and can be addressed individually
using servo IDs.

This gave me a platform for experimenting with servo communication, robot
joint control, motion recording, coordinate systems and eventually
higher-level autonomous control.

## Electronics

One of the first parts of the project was understanding how to correctly
power and communicate with multiple relatively high-current servos.

The arm uses:

- 8 × Feetech STS3215 serial bus servos
- Waveshare Serial Bus Servo Driver
- 12 V, 10 A power supply
- Serial communication between the computer/controller and servo bus

Using serial bus servos was particularly interesting because multiple
actuators can share the same communication bus while still being individually
addressed. I worked with servo IDs, position commands and servo feedback
rather than treating the actuators as simple PWM motors.

This also introduced me to an important distinction in robotics between the
control electronics and the power electronics. The servos require
substantially more current than the control electronics can provide, meaning
the servo power system has to be designed separately while still maintaining
reliable communication between the controller and actuators.

## Python servo control

I began controlling the arm through Python using an STS3215-compatible Python
library. The goal was to create software that could communicate directly with
each servo and allow me to read and command individual joint positions.

This involved learning how the servo bus worked, identifying individual
servos using their IDs and converting between the servo's internal position
representation and useful joint movements. Rather than only creating
predetermined movements, I wanted the software architecture to eventually
allow higher-level programs to control the arm.

## Motion recording and replay

One of the features I experimented with was recording and replaying robot
movements. The idea was to capture the positions of each joint at different
points in a movement and store these positions as a trajectory. The arm could
then replay the recorded sequence by commanding each servo through the saved
joint positions.

This introduced me to the difference between simply commanding a robot to a
position and controlling an entire trajectory. It also raised additional
problems such as movement speed, synchronising multiple joints, interpolation
between recorded positions and producing smooth motion rather than moving
abruptly between individual poses.

## Robot model and URDF

As the project developed, I also began creating a URDF representation of the
robot. The purpose of the URDF was to describe the arm as a system of links
and joints rather than simply a collection of servo IDs — my introduction to
representing a physical robot mathematically in software.

Each servo corresponds to a joint, while the printed mechanical components
form links connecting those joints. Creating the model helped me understand
concepts such as joint axes, link lengths, joint limits, coordinate frames,
parent and child links, and end-effector position and orientation. This
connected the mechanical construction of the arm with the electronics and
software controlling it.

## End-effector orientation

One problem I became interested in was maintaining a particular orientation
of the end effector while moving the arm. For example, if the shoulder and
elbow joints move, the orientation of the wrist naturally changes. If I want
the end effector to remain perpendicular to the desk, another joint has to
compensate for those movements.

This turned what initially looked like a simple servo-control problem into a
kinematics problem. It introduced me to the idea that controlling a robotic
arm at the joint level and controlling it in Cartesian space are fundamentally
different problems. Instead of saying "move servo 2 to this angle", I
eventually wanted to be able to say "move the end effector here while
maintaining this orientation" — which requires calculating the joint
configuration necessary to produce the requested pose.

## Mechanical learning

Although this was primarily an electronics project, building the physical arm
forced me to learn a significant amount of mechanical engineering along the
way. I 3D printed the existing arm design and assembled the mechanism myself.

Working with the physical robot helped me understand how factors such as
servo placement, joint geometry, link length, mechanical tolerances, stiffness
and weight distribution directly affect the electronics and control system.
It also showed me that a robot which works correctly in software does not
necessarily work correctly in the real world — backlash, printed-part
tolerances, servo limitations, wiring, friction and the mass of the arm all
influence its actual behaviour. That interaction between mechanical,
electrical and software systems became one of the most useful parts of the
project.

## Debugging the servo bus

The project also gave me experience troubleshooting an actual hardware
communication failure. At one point, my software stopped detecting the
servos when scanning the serial bus. Instead of immediately assuming the code
was wrong, I had to work through the complete signal chain: computer → servo
controller → serial bus → servo → power system.

This meant considering power delivery, wiring, serial communication, servo
IDs, controller configuration and whether the Waveshare interface itself had
failed. The debugging eventually led me to suspect a problem with the servo
driver/controller board. Although this stopped progress on parts of the
project, it was also a useful lesson in electronics debugging: software,
communications, power and hardware all have to be considered together.

## Longer-term goal: desktop robot assistant

My longer-term goal was to use the arm as a platform for experimenting with
intelligent robotics. Rather than creating a robot that only executes
predetermined movements, I wanted to investigate how perception and AI could
be connected to physical hardware — a camera to recognise objects around my
desk, pointing toward tools or components, responding to voice commands and
eventually performing simple autonomous actions.

The architecture I was considering separated the system into two levels. A
higher-level computer, such as a Raspberry Pi, Jetson or PC, would handle
tasks such as computer vision, voice processing and AI. The lower-level
robot-control system would then translate those decisions into commands for
the individual joints:

Voice command → AI/perception → desired robot action → robot pose/trajectory
→ joint commands → serial servo bus → physical movement

This part of the project remains largely experimental and is something I want
to return to.

## What I actually built / worked on

The completed and experimental work on the project included physically 3D
printing and assembling the robot arm, installing and wiring eight serial bus
servos, setting up the servo controller and external power system,
communicating with STS3215 servos through Python, experimenting with
individual joint control, investigating servo position feedback, working on
motion recording and replay, creating a URDF representation of the arm,
exploring end-effector orientation constraints, and troubleshooting
communication failures on the servo bus.

The mechanical design itself was sourced online rather than designed by me.
However, manufacturing and assembling it gave me practical experience with
mechanical systems that I had not originally expected from what began
primarily as an electronics project.

## What I want to do next

The project is unfinished, and there are several areas I want to return to:

- Repair or replace the failed servo communication hardware.
- Restore reliable communication with all eight STS3215 servos.
- Finish and validate the URDF against the physical robot.
- Create a cleaner Python control interface for the complete arm.
- Improve trajectory recording and playback.
- Implement smooth interpolation between poses.
- Explore forward and inverse kinematics.
- Maintain end-effector orientation automatically during movement.
- Add a camera for object detection and localisation.
- Experiment with voice commands.
- Connect higher-level AI decisions to robot actions.
- Investigate autonomous object interaction.
- Potentially use the arm as a platform for reinforcement learning or
  imitation learning.

## What I learned

The biggest lesson from this project was how interconnected robotics
disciplines are. I started the project mainly because I wanted to learn more
about electronics and robot control. However, getting a physical arm working
required me to deal with electronics, power delivery, serial communication,
programming, mechanical assembly, coordinate systems and robot kinematics.

It was also one of my first experiences of the gap between controlling
something in software and making the same system work reliably in the
physical world. Even though the project is unfinished, it became a useful
foundation for the robotics projects I have worked on since.
